## Quick context

Rina runs operations at a 14-person creative agency. She does not write most final client work, but she reviews briefs, scopes, launch notes, and client-facing recaps before they leave the team. She is not a daily power user, but she is the person active users need to pull into drafts before publishing.

## Transcript

**[00:00] Sarah:** Thanks for making time. I know you are not the person drafting in Noted every day, so I am especially curious where it enters your workflow.

**[00:09] Rina:** That is exactly my relationship with it. I am usually not starting the document. I am the person someone pings when the document is almost ready and they want me to catch the obvious problem before a client sees it.

**[00:22] Sarah:** What kind of document is that usually?

**[00:25] Rina:** Client recaps, campaign briefs, launch checklists, sometimes a positioning doc. Anything where the writer has been in the weeds and needs someone with distance.

**[00:36] Sarah:** When someone uses Noted to draft, how do they bring you in today?

**[00:40] Rina:** Usually they paste the draft into Slack or move it into Google Docs. Sometimes they publish a Noted page and send me the link, but that feels like the wrong mode.

**[00:51] Sarah:** Wrong how?

**[00:53] Rina:** Publish feels finished. Even if they say "ignore the roughness," the format tells my brain this is a thing I should judge as close to final.

**[01:04] Sarah:** What would feel more accurate?

**[01:06] Rina:** Review. Comment. Sanity check. Something that says, "this is still owned by the writer, but your reaction matters."

**[01:17] Sarah:** Do you need edit access?

**[01:19] Rina:** Almost never. I actually prefer not having it. If I can edit, I start fixing sentences. If I can only comment, I stay at the level they asked for: "the client will ask about budget here" or "this order is confusing."

**[01:34] Sarah:** So comment-only is not a limitation. It is a guardrail.

**[01:38] Rina:** Yes. Especially with junior people. They need to keep ownership of the draft. If I rewrite it, they learn less and I become a bottleneck.

**[01:49] Sarah:** That is useful. Tell me about the last time this happened.

**[01:53] Rina:** Last week. One of our strategists used Noted and Coworker to turn a call transcript into a launch brief. The structure was pretty good, but the risk section was too soft. She sent me a public link and said, "can you review this before I send it to the client?"

**[02:12] Sarah:** What did you do?

**[02:14] Rina:** I read it, then copied three chunks into Slack with comments. That is a terrible review artifact, but it was the fastest way to respond.

**[02:25] Sarah:** What got lost?

**[02:27] Rina:** Anchoring. My Slack comment was no longer attached to the paragraph. Two days later she asked which section I meant, and I had to reconstruct it.

**[02:37] Sarah:** Did you want to see the Coworker chat that produced the brief?

**[02:41] Rina:** Sometimes. Not the whole thing. But if the AI suggested a structure, and I am about to challenge that structure, I want to know whether the writer chose it or just accepted it.

**[02:55] Sarah:** That sounds like context preservation more than collaboration.

**[02:59] Rina:** Exactly. Collaboration is the big word. The smaller problem is: keep the review attached to the thinking.

**[03:09] Sarah:** If the product had presence, cursors, and live editing, would that solve your problem?

**[03:15] Rina:** Not really. We are rarely in the document at the same time. Our review is asynchronous. I need precise comments and clear permissions, not a feeling that we are sitting together.

**[03:29] Sarah:** What permissions would you expect?

**[03:31] Rina:** Owner, commenter, maybe viewer. Editor later, but not first. For the use case I described, commenter is the star.

**[03:42] Sarah:** Do you care whether the reviewer has an account?

**[03:45] Rina:** It depends. For my internal team, an account is fine. For a client, no. If we ask a client to make an account just to react to a paragraph, the writer will move the whole thing to Docs.

**[03:59] Sarah:** So the same document might need internal review and external reaction.

**[04:03] Rina:** Yes, and those are different trust levels. Internal people can see more context. Clients should see a cleaner surface.

**[04:12] Sarah:** That is a sharp boundary.

**[04:14] Rina:** It matters. Our drafts contain messy notes, pricing thoughts, sometimes "do not say this to client" bullets. I do not want a share setting that accidentally exposes the workshop.

**[04:28] Sarah:** Does that fear make people avoid sharing from Noted?

**[04:31] Rina:** Definitely. If you are not sure what a link exposes, you do not send the link. You copy-paste the safe parts elsewhere.

**[04:41] Sarah:** That would show up as low publish or low share usage, even if the draft was valuable.

**[04:47] Rina:** Yes. The value is happening before the share. The leak happens at the boundary.

**[04:55] Sarah:** What do you think of the AI Coworker itself?

**[04:58] Rina:** It is good at making the first version less blank. I would not trust it to decide the final client framing, but I do trust it to organize raw material.

**[05:09] Sarah:** Do you ever want to comment on the AI's contribution specifically?

**[05:13] Rina:** Yes. Sometimes my feedback is really, "this sounds like AI structure, not our judgment." I want to say that without making it personal to the writer.

**[05:25] Sarah:** Interesting. Does model attribution matter there?

**[05:28] Rina:** Maybe not the exact model name, but some indication of "this came from the assistant" versus "the writer wrote this" would change how I review. I am gentler when I know I am reacting to an AI draft.

**[05:43] Sarah:** Do you use Squad agents?

**[05:45] Rina:** No. I understand the idea, but I would not know how to set one up for our agency. We have patterns, but they are fuzzy.

**[05:55] Sarah:** What would make Squad easier?

**[05:58] Rina:** Start with a real workflow. "Client recap reviewer." "Launch brief reviewer." Not "create an agent." That phrasing makes me feel like I am programming.

**[06:10] Sarah:** So templates over blank configuration.

**[06:13] Rina:** Yes. And honestly, connect it to the review problem. If a Squad agent could pre-check a brief before I reviewed it, I would care. If it is just a bot I have to configure, I do not.

**[06:27] Sarah:** Let me test something. If Noted shipped only one collaboration feature first, what would you choose?

**[06:34] Rina:** Comment-only review links with clear scope. I can say exactly who can see what, and the comments stay attached.

**[06:44] Sarah:** Not editor roles?

**[06:46] Rina:** Later.

**[06:48] Sarah:** Not presence?

**[06:50] Rina:** Much later.

**[06:52] Sarah:** Not shared workspaces?

**[06:54] Rina:** Useful for account management, not the first pain.

**[07:00] Sarah:** What would success look like for you?

**[07:03] Rina:** My strategist sends a link, I leave five comments, she resolves them, and the brief becomes client-ready without leaving Noted.

**[07:13] Sarah:** How often would that happen?

**[07:15] Rina:** Three to five times a week across the team. More in launch season.

**[07:21] Sarah:** And if we do not solve it?

**[07:23] Rina:** Noted stays a drafting tool, not a workflow tool. That might still be valuable, but it means the final mile always happens somewhere else.

**[07:35] Sarah:** Final mile is a useful phrase.

**[07:37] Rina:** It is exactly that. The AI gets you from zero to seventy. The missing review loop gets you from seventy to shipped.

**[07:47] Sarah:** Anything I did not ask that I should have?

**[07:50] Rina:** Ask whether people trust the share boundary. Because if they do not, the rest of collaboration does not matter.

**[07:59] Sarah:** Do you?

**[08:01] Rina:** Today? Not enough to use it for client-facing review. Internally maybe. Externally no.

**[08:09] Sarah:** That is clear. Thank you.

**[08:11] Rina:** Happy to help. Please ship comments before cursors.
